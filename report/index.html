Tracking

The numbers are not real yet

Do this before anything else on this page

If Meta reports materially more revenue than your store banked, every other finding is a read on fiction. Fix the reporting, then diagnose.

The signature: platform revenue running well ahead of banked revenue, over the same dates.

What to do

  • Change your reporting to 7-day click. Ads Manager, Columns, Compare attribution settings.
  • Do not change your optimisation window. 1-day view feeds the conversion model and removing it makes delivery worse. This is a reporting change only.
  • Exclude existing customers on cold campaigns, then check whether platform ROAS lands near your acquisition MER.
  • If account MER says 3 and Meta says 9, open the attribution breakdown, find the column that equals 3, and use only that column from now on.
  • Re-run the diagnostic once the two numbers agree.

What not to do

  • Brief a creative round off the current numbers.
  • Scale anything that looks like a winner on the inflated figure.
  • Strip 1-day view out of the campaign itself. That is a different change and it hurts.

The tell. One audited ad reported 12.84 ROAS. On new audiences it was 5. On incremental attribution it was 1.71. Same ad, three lenses. Another account was raising budget on an ad set running 70% view-through.

Media buying

You turned off ads that were carrying the account

The signature: you recently killed ads that were taking a lot of spend because their ad-level ROAS looked poor.

What to do

  • Turn them back on. Same targets, same budgets.
  • Judge ads on amount spent, not ad-level ROAS. Meta decides where spend goes and it is rarely wrong about that.
  • Move your KPI up a level, to ad set or campaign, where attribution is reliable.
  • Set a rule: never kill a low-ROAS ad inside an ad set that is hitting its target.

What not to do

  • Replace them with new creative. The creative was not the problem.
  • Judge the replacements on ad-level ROAS either.

The tell. Meta attributes on last click but optimises across the whole click sequence, so a high-spending, mediocre-ROAS ad is often doing work a later ad gets credited for. For one operator running eight-figure spend, switching the killed high-spenders back on is the first move on any new account, and it almost always recovers around 30% of spend at the same target.

Your budget cannot judge this many ads

The signature: more than a couple of live ads got under $50 last week.

What to do

  • Cap live ad sets at daily budget divided by CPA. At $2,000 a day and a $50 CPA that is 40. Most accounts are well past their own number.
  • Queue, do not launch. New creative waits for a slot to open.
  • Three ads per ad set, and all three must be versions of one concept, so you learn which idea won rather than which edit won.
  • Weekly, check the floor rather than the ceiling: how many live ads got under $50 this week?

What not to do

  • Clean up and repeat the cycle next month. Launching fewer is the fix, not tidying more.
  • Read a starved ad as a loser. It was never funded enough to be either.

The tell. A real account ran 186 ad objects on $141 a day, which is 76 cents per ad per day. 151 of them never cleared $15 and produced three purchases between them. An ad at that rate accumulates $23 in a month. It can never be judged.

You are paying more to reach the same people

The signature: CPM up, and purchases per 1,000 impressions flat or down.

What to do

  • Check overlap first, because it is measurable. Sum reach across your campaigns and compare it to account-level reach. Audits find 80% overlap.
  • The tell for overlap: many campaigns, few ad sets each, lots of creative, moderate spend. Consolidate.
  • If overlap is clean, widen the pool. New geos, new audiences, new placements.
  • Check your own campaigns are not bidding against each other before you blame the auction.

What not to do

  • Brief a creative round. The ads are earning their clicks.
  • Raise budgets into a saturating pool. That is what made it expensive.

The tell. A high CPM is not automatically bad. If purchases per 1,000 impressions rose with it, the auction found better people and you are getting what you paid for. Check that before you treat cost as a problem.

Your bidding is buying your cheapest customer

The signature: average order value fell and you did not change the offer.

What to do

  • Check whether a high-value and a low-value product share an ad set. If they do, separate them.
  • Volume-optimised bidding has no reference to order value, so it routes spend to whichever converts cheaper. That is almost always the cheap one.
  • If you have a real spread in order value, a value-based target is worth testing. If your catalogue is one price point, it is not.
  • Re-read the ads on your high-value SKU after separating. They usually were not the problem.

What not to do

  • Kill the ads on your expensive product because they "underperform". They were outbid, not outperformed.
  • Set a cost cap without reference to what you actually realise per order.

The tell. Measured on one account switching bid strategy on identical creative: average order value rose 11.5% across all 177 ad sets. Same ads, different bidding, a different customer walked in.

Creative

The ad is not earning the click

The signature: click rate was below the ads beside it from the day it launched.

What to do

  • A new concept, not a new edit. A different argument, not a different crop.
  • Test in this order: angle, then offer, then avatar, then format. Format is the last axis and it is where most people start.
  • Pull the language from reviews and ad comments. The words they use beat the words you would choose.
  • Check you are speaking at the right awareness level. Benefits do not land on someone who does not know they have the problem.

What not to do

  • Rewrite the landing page. Nothing after the click has been reached yet.
  • Chase a higher hook rate as the goal. It does not correlate with conversion.
  • Compare your click rate to a number from a podcast.

The tell. Click rate means nothing across accounts. Profitable accounts run at 0.5% and at 3%. It only means something against the ads sitting next to it.

Fatigue

The signature: it started strong and decayed while frequency climbed, with only a handful of distinct ideas running.

What to do

  • New versions of the thing that worked, plus genuinely distinct concepts running beside them.
  • Keep the winning variable fixed and change what surrounds it.
  • Watch the direction frequency moves on this ad against its own history. The level is meaningless without the window and the spend attached.

What not to do

  • Scrap the concept. It earned its decay.
  • Add more edits of the same execution. They will be grouped with it and reach nobody new.

The tell. Frequency has no universal number. A 3 on $2k of spend in a week is a different animal to a 3 on $1m in a month. Read the direction, never the level.

Volume without variety

The signature: plenty of ads running, frequency still climbing, reach flat.

What to do

  • Fewer, more different. One genuinely new angle beats ten new edits.
  • If two ads share a hook, a claim and a format, count them as one ad. That is roughly how the platform counts them too.
  • Check reach, not ad count. If reach is flat while ad count rises, the new ads are not reaching anyone new.
  • Diversify along angle first, then offer, then avatar, then format.

What not to do

  • Launch more variations. That is the thing that stopped working.
  • Blame audience size before checking whether your ads are actually distinct.

The tell. One account ran 200 near-identical ads that grouped into about 20 entities. The ten inside each group served the same pool with zero unique reach. That is why tripling output changes nothing.

Funnel

One step drops off

The signature: they clicked, and one transition after the click is clearly worse than the rest.

What to do

  • Fix the step, not the ad. Add to cart failing is a product page or price problem. Checkout failing is a shipping, trust or mechanics problem.
  • As a shape, add to cart, checkout and purchase run about 4:2:1. A step far off that ratio is the one to open.
  • Judge cold traffic separately. A 2.5% blended store conversion rate is often 0.8 to 1.5% on cold.
  • Check the ad and the page make the same promise. Congruence, not just quality.

What not to do

  • Brief new creative. The creative already won the click.
  • Use blended conversion rate to set your break-even. It fails at scale.

The tell. Your site conversion rate also sets your ad costs, because the platform prices you partly on expected action rate. One account took CVR from 2% to 4% and CPC fell from $3 to $2, CPA from $150 to $50. The funnel was making the media look expensive.

Nothing converts well

The signature: no single step fails. Every transition is mediocre.

What to do

  • Look above the page. When everything is mediocre rather than one thing broken, the cause is usually what is being sold or the terms it is sold on.
  • Run the ablation test. One brand turned off ads, then email, then flows, then partnerships, and demand held. They changed the offer and demand halved instantly. That is how you find out what was actually carrying it.
  • Check margin and order value against your acquisition cost before touching the ads again.

What not to do

  • Order another creative batch. You already know creative is not the variable.
  • A/B test button colours on a page nobody wanted to be on.

The tell. Test hit rate maps cleanly. Under 10% points above the account entirely. 10 to 20% is angles. 20 to 30% is execution. Above 30% and still stuck is a media buying problem, not a creative one.

Not yours

This looks like demand

The signature: performance faded and frequency stayed flat.

What to do

  • Confirm it against the same period last year, and against a second channel.
  • Check whether anything changed off-platform. A promotion ending, a PR spike stopping, an email flow paused.
  • Hold your structure. Do not rebuild an account around a demand dip.
  • If it is a season you know, plan the offer for it rather than the creative.

What not to do

  • Rebuild the creative library. It was not the creative.
  • Cut spend hard and lose the compounding when demand returns.

The tell. Creative fatigue has a fingerprint, and it is decay with rising frequency. Decay on flat frequency is demand-side. One brand's decay reversed entirely on a single product drop, inside an unchanged campaign structure.
